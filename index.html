            The physics behind Project Lorentz

            How a magnetic field turns a small voltage into a fast particle

            A cyclotron gives a charged particle many small pushes instead of one huge one. The magnetic field is what makes this possible: it bends the particle into a circle and brings it back to the same accelerating gap again and again, always at the right moment.

            The project is named after the Lorentz force, F = q(E + v × B): the force on a charge q in an electric field E and a magnetic field B. In a cyclotron the electric part pushes the particle faster each time it crosses the gap, and the magnetic part bends its path into a circle.

            Built by Leonel, Michael, Gabriel and Marco

            How to use the lab

            1. Build the magnet. In Electromagnet, choose the number of coils, the turns per coil, the current I and the pole gap g, or switch to Set the field B. Or load a real machine from Load a machine.
            2. Set up the accelerator. Choose the dee radius R and the dee voltage V. Keep Fixed frequency for a classic cyclotron, or let the frequency follow the particle (a synchrocyclotron).
            3. Pick a particle and press play. Use the speed buttons, drag the timeline, or press Skip to end. After a run, Replay 0.1× shows the last orbits in slow motion with every push labelled.
            4. Make it bigger. Drag the edge of the copper dees (or the amber knob on it) to change the size of the machine itself: the dee radius R. Drag the striped corner of the view to make the picture bigger or smaller; double-click it to go back to automatic size. Scroll the mouse wheel or pinch to zoom in on any spot, and drag to move around.
            5. Ride along. Press Ride the proton (or V) to see the machine from just behind the particle. You hear a thump every time it crosses the gap and gets a push, and a tick at every peak of the voltage. While the two stay in step, the beat is steady: that is the cyclotron resonance. The particle's own clock is shown next to the lab clock; it runs slow by the factor γ, as relativity predicts.
            6. Read the numbers. The tiles show the essentials; the tabs explain every quantity. Turn on Show working to see each formula with your numbers in it. Record saves a run to the Notebook so you can copy a results table.
            7. Experiment. Magnetic field chaos lets you flip, scale or shake the field while the particle flies. Overload mode lets you push the machine past its limits.
            8. Explore the other screens: fire a Gauss gun, race particles, collide them head-on, and fire them into targets. Each screen has its own missions.
            9. Simple or Full detail. The switch at the bottom right changes how much you see. Simple hides the expert controls, adds a plain-language "What is happening" note on every screen and puts ? buttons next to the controls. Full shows all the theory and the working.
            10. Keyboard: Space play/pause, → step, V ride along. The buttons at the bottom right switch sound, colour scheme and full screen.

            What is a cyclotron?

            Ernest Lawrence and his student M. Stanley Livingston built the first working cyclotrons at Berkeley. In January 1931 Livingston's 4-inch machine accelerated hydrogen molecular ions (H₂⁺) to 80 keV using only about 1 kV. Within months their 11-inch machine passed 1 MeV: 1.1 MeV protons in August 1931, and 1.22 MeV by January 1932. Lawrence received the 1939 Nobel Prize in Physics for the invention.

            Inside a flat vacuum chamber sit two hollow, D-shaped metal electrodes called dees, with a narrow gap between them. The chamber sits between the poles of a large electromagnet, so a uniform magnetic field passes straight through it.

            An alternating radio-frequency (RF) voltage is connected across the dees. Every time the particle crosses the gap, the voltage pushes it forward. Inside a dee there is no electric field, only the magnetic field, which bends the particle around a half circle and sends it back to the gap. By then the voltage has flipped, so it is pushed again. Faster means a bigger circle, so the path becomes a spiral.

            1 · Making the magnetic field: the electromagnet

            The field comes from copper coils wound around two iron poles. The iron carries the magnetic field around a closed loop, so almost all of the coils' effort is spent pushing the field across the air gap where the dees sit.

            Ampère's circuital law says that the magnetic field "added up" around a closed loop equals μ₀ times the current threading the loop. Taking the loop through the iron and across the gap, and treating the iron as perfect (μr ≫ 1):

            B = μ₀ N I / g N = (number of coils) × (turns per coil) · I = current (A) · g = pole gap (m) · μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A
            • Double the turns or double the current and B doubles: only the product NI (ampere-turns) matters, because coils in series all carry the same current.
            • Halve the gap and B doubles, but the dees need room to sit in the gap.
            • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows; your thumb points along B (right-hand grip rule).
            • The limit: iron saturates near 2 T. Stronger machines use superconducting coils. Our formula ignores saturation, so the lab warns you above 2 T.

            Check: a 1.5 T field in a 50 mm gap needs NI = Bg/μ₀ ≈ 59 700 ampere-turns, the same figure CERN's accelerator school gives for this example.

            2 · The magnetic force on a moving charge

            A charge q moving with velocity v through a magnetic field B feels the magnetic part of the Lorentz force:

            F = q v × B  ⟹  F = |q|vB sin θ In a cyclotron v is always at 90° to B, so sin θ = 1 and F = |q|vB.
            • The force is perpendicular to both v and B (Fleming's left-hand rule for a positive charge). For a negative charge the force is reversed, so electrons circle the other way.
            • Because the force is always at 90° to the motion, it does no work: it changes the particle's direction but never its speed. That is why the speed graph is flat while the particle is inside a dee.

            3 · Circular motion: the radius of the orbit

            The magnetic force provides exactly the centripetal force needed for circular motion:

            |q|vB = mv² / r  ⟹  r = mv / (|q|B) = p / (|q|B) A faster particle (bigger momentum p) moves on a bigger circle. A stronger field makes the circle smaller.

            Each push at the gap increases v, so each half circle is a little larger than the last. Since the energy goes up by the same amount at each crossing and r ∝ v ∝ √K, the orbits get closer together as the particle moves outward.

            4 · The cyclotron frequency: the key idea

            T = 2πr / v = 2πm / (|q|B)  ⟹  f = |q|B / (2πm) The speed v cancels out. For a proton, f ≈ 15.2 MHz for every tesla of field.

            A fast particle travels a bigger circle in exactly the same time as a slow particle travels a small one. So an RF voltage with a fixed frequency equal to f arrives in step with the particle at every crossing: the resonance condition, fRF = f. Set the RF frequency offset to +2 % and watch the particle drift out of step.

            5 · Energy from the gap

            ΔK = |q|V cos ψ   (maximum |q|V when ψ = 0) ψ is the timing: how far the voltage is from its peak when the particle crosses. Two crossings per revolution give up to 2|q|V per turn.

            A proton (charge e) crossing 200 kV gains 200 keV; an alpha particle (2e) gains 400 keV. 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

            6 · Maximum energy, number of turns and time

            vmax = |q|BR / m    Kmax = q²B²R² / (2m) The dee voltage V does not appear: V only decides how many turns it takes.
            N = Kmax / (2|q|V)    t = N × T = πBR² / (2V) Surprise: the acceleration time does not depend on the particle's mass or charge. The Grand Prix race on the Race screen shows this.

            Because Kmax ∝ q²/m, an alpha particle (q = 2e, m ≈ 4 u) reaches almost the same energy as a proton in the same machine.

            7 · The relativistic limit, and how engineers beat it

            Near the speed of light a particle behaves as if its mass were γm, with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). The revolution frequency f = |q|B/(2πγm) drops as the particle speeds up. With a fixed RF frequency each half-turn arrives a little later, the pushes get weaker, and finally the voltage points the wrong way.

            Classic fixed-frequency cyclotrons topped out at roughly 20–25 MeV for protons. Electrons are hopeless: γ = 2 already at 511 keV.

            • Synchrocyclotron: lower the RF frequency as the particle gains energy (the lab's Follows particle mode).
            • Isochronous cyclotron: make B grow with radius exactly as γ grows. TRIUMF and most modern medical cyclotrons work this way.
            • Synchrotron: keep the radius fixed and ramp up B. The LHC uses 1232 superconducting bending magnets at about 8.3 T.

            Why racers win, lose or tie

            Magnetic sprint. Every racer is pushed through the same gate, then a uniform field bends it into a half circle back onto the detector plate. The time for half a circle is

            t = πm / (|q|B)   and the landing distance is 2r = 2mv / (|q|B) The speed cancels out of the time. Only the charge-to-mass ratio q/m decides the winner.
            • The electron's q/m is about 1836 times the proton's, so it wins by an enormous margin.
            • An alpha particle has half the proton's q/m, so it takes about twice as long.
            • A deuteron, an alpha particle and a carbon-12 nucleus all have q/m ≈ e/(2 u). Their race is a photo finish decided by tiny mass differences that come from nuclear binding energy (E = mc²): carbon-12 edges out the alpha, which edges out the deuteron.
            • This is exactly how a mass spectrometer separates particles: different q/m, different landing points.

            Cyclotron Grand Prix. With identical machines and the RF tuned to each racer, the time to reach the edge is t = πBR²/(2V) for every particle, so the race is almost a dead heat. Relativity, and fixed-frequency machines losing step, decide what is left.

            Collisions: energy, barriers and new particles

            Collision energy. What matters is the energy in the centre-of-mass frame, the invariant mass

            Ecm = √[(E₁ + E₂)² − (p₁c − p₂c)²] E = K + mc² is each particle's total energy. The energy available to make new particles is Q = Ecm − m₁c² − m₂c².
            • Colliders beat fixed targets. Two 150 MeV protons meeting head-on give Q = 300 MeV. To get the same from a proton hitting a proton at rest, the beam would need about 650 MeV. The Collider screen shows this "fixed-target equivalent" for every shot.
            • The Coulomb barrier. Two positive nuclei repel. Head-on, they get no closer than rmin = k z₁z₂e²/Q (with ke² = 1.44 MeV·fm). Unless Q is above about 1.44 z₁z₂ / (R₁ + R₂) MeV, with nuclear radii R ≈ 1.2 A1/3 fm, they bounce off without touching.
            • New particles need energy (E = mc²): a neutral pion needs 135 MeV, a π⁺π⁻ pair 279 MeV, and a proton–antiproton pair 1877 MeV.
            • Annihilation. An electron and a positron destroy each other and turn into gamma rays, each about 511 keV at low energy. This is the real "explosion" of matter into light, and the basis of PET scanners.
            • The lab's events conserve energy, momentum and charge exactly; which particles appear, and their directions, are chosen at random within those rules, as in real collisions.

            Stopping in matter: the Bragg peak

            A fast charged particle loses energy mainly by knocking electrons in the material. The Bethe formula gives the energy lost per centimetre:

            −dE/dx = K z² (Z/A) ρ (1/β²) [½ ln(2mec²β²γ²Wmax/I²) − β²] K = 0.307 MeV cm²/mol · z = particle charge · ρ, Z/A, I = density, electrons per nucleon mass, mean excitation energy of the material.
            • The 1/β² factor means a slow particle loses energy faster. So the loss rises sharply just before the particle stops: the Bragg peak.
            • This is why proton therapy works: choose the energy so the Bragg peak lands in the tumour, sparing tissue in front and behind. Therapy accelerators deliver 70–250 MeV protons.
            • Alpha particles (z = 2) lose energy 4 times faster at the same speed, so a sheet of paper stops them. Electrons are light, scatter a lot and have no sharp Bragg peak.
            • The lab's ranges agree with the NIST PSTAR tables to within about 1 % for protons in water (10–250 MeV). Heat, glow and damage on the Target Range are illustrative.

            The Gauss gun: throwing things with magnetism

            Two different machines share the name. The classic Gauss rifle uses permanent magnets and steel balls, with no electricity at all. A coil gun (also called a reluctance launcher) uses electromagnets switched on one after another. Many people call the coil gun a Gauss gun too, although strictly the magnet-and-ball device came first. The Gauss gun screen has both.

            Coil gun 1: each coil is an electromagnet

            Current flowing round a coil makes a magnetic field along its axis, strongest in the middle. For a long, thin coil of length ℓ:

            B ≈ μ₀ N I / ℓ The same idea as the cyclotron's electromagnet: more turns or more current, stronger field. The lab's coils are short and fat (more turns means more layers of wire), so it uses the exact formula for the field on the axis of a thick coil.

            The field magnetises the iron slug: the tiny magnetic domains inside it line up, and the slug becomes a magnet whose near end is pulled towards the coil. The magnetisation M can only grow up to a limit, saturation (about 2.15 T for soft iron). Once saturated, more current makes the coil stronger but the slug no stronger, so doubling the current gives much less than double the speed. Aluminium has no domains to line up, so this kind of gun cannot pull it.

            Coil gun 2: the force and the timing

            F = μ₀ M A [H(front) − H(back)] A = cross-section of the slug, H = the coil's field strength at the slug's two ends (B = μ₀H). The pull is biggest where the field changes fastest, as the slug enters the coil, and zero when the slug sits in the middle.
            • Suck-back. Once the slug passes the coil's middle, the same pull points backwards. A well-timed gun switches each coil off as the slug reaches the middle. In multi-stage guns each coil is switched by a sensor that tracks the slug, and the switching speed and timing accuracy of the electronics are principal design constraints.
            • Real coils are slow. A coil has inductance L, so its current takes about τ = L/R to build up and to die away. With "Current takes time to build up" on, the best switch-off point moves before the middle.
            • Each stage adds less speed. Every coil does about the same work W on the slug, so ½mv² grows by W per coil and the speed grows like √(number of coils). A faster slug also spends less time in each coil.
            • Efficiency is low. η = ½mv² / Ein. Most of the energy heats the copper (P = I²R). A carefully optimised two-stage laboratory system reached a gross efficiency of only (1.36 ± 0.02) %. The lab's simpler model (no capacitor losses, eddy currents, hysteresis or back-EMF) gives anything from under 1 % to about 15 % depending on the settings: for a given coil, an upper estimate.
            • Speed. Published reluctance coil guns mostly reach roughly 10 to 40 m/s; a research team reached 75 m/s with two stages and 130 m/s with a many-stage launcher.

            The magnet-and-ball Gauss rifle

            A magnet pulls a steel ball very strongly when it is close, and hardly at all a few ball-widths away. The incoming ball falls into this deep "energy well" and arrives at full speed. The bump passes through the magnet and the balls behind it, and the last ball flies off. It sat several radii from the magnet, in a shallow part of the well, so it needs only a little energy to escape.

            Kout = η (Kin + U꜀) − U꜀ / (2k − 1)⁴ U꜀ = depth of the well at the magnet face, k = balls behind each magnet (the last one is (2k − 1) radii away), η = 0.9 = share of the energy passed on in the bump. The lab models the well as U(d) = −U꜀(r/d)⁴ and sets U꜀ so that one stage reproduces measured speeds.
            MagnetSteel ballMeasured exit speed, one stage
            D44¼ inch6.2 mph (2.77 m/s)
            D66⅜ inch3.1 mph (1.39 m/s)
            D88½ inch2.9 mph (1.30 m/s)
            DAA⅝ inch2.6 mph (1.16 m/s)
            DCC¾ inch3.3 mph (1.48 m/s)
            • Bigger is not faster: the smallest ball was the fastest in K&J Magnetics' high-speed camera tests, because a bigger ball's mass grows faster than the magnet's pull.
            • One ball behind the magnet is not enough. It touches the magnet and is held as tightly as the incoming ball was pulled in, so the bump cannot free it.
            • Each stage adds less speed: every bump wastes a share of the incoming energy, and a faster ball brings more to waste. The speed creeps towards a limit where the loss equals the gain.
            • Not free energy. The energy was stored when the balls were stuck to the magnets. Pulling them off to reset the rifle takes at least as much work as the shot released.

            Measuring the speed with a ballistic pendulum

            v = (m + M)/m × √(2gh) The projectile (mass m) sticks in a hanging block (mass M): momentum is conserved in the impact, then the block's kinetic energy turns into height h. The lab's chronograph measures the same speed with two light gates 5 cm apart.

            Coil heating uses P = I²R and the copper's heat capacity; the cooling, the burn-out at 180 °C, the smoke and the tumbling cans are illustrative.

            Can a particle accelerator explode?

            Not like a bomb. A particle beam contains a tiny amount of matter, and collisions between particles cannot set off a chain reaction. The real danger is stored energy:

            • Magnets. Superconducting magnets store huge energy. If one suddenly stops superconducting (a quench), that energy turns into heat and the liquid helium boils. On 19 September 2008 a faulty electrical connection between two LHC magnets caused an electrical arc that punctured the helium enclosure: about six tonnes of liquid helium escaped, damaging magnets and breaking their anchors in the concrete floor. The LHC was shut down for over a year of repairs.
            • Beams. Each full LHC beam stores about 362 MJ, enough to melt around 500 kg of copper. That is why the beam is steered into a graphite "beam dump" when it has to be stopped.
            • High voltage and current. Sparks across the gap, and coils overheating without cooling, can damage a machine. Interlocks switch things off first.

            The explosions, shaking and flashing in Overload mode and on the Target Range are deliberately exaggerated, illustrative effects.

            Real cyclotrons and what they are used for

            80 keV

            Livingston's 4-inch cyclotron at Berkeley, January 1931: the first working machine accelerated H₂⁺ ions with only about 1 kV.

            10–20 MeV

            Protons for making fluorine-18, the tracer in most PET scans. McMaster University, for example, runs a 16.5 MeV machine.

            70–250 MeV

            Protons for cancer therapy. A higher energy reaches a deeper tumour, where the protons deposit most of their dose (the Bragg peak).

            5.7 T

            Peak field of IBA's S2C2, a superconducting synchrocyclotron about 2.5 m across that delivers 230 MeV protons for therapy after about 40,000 turns.

            18 m · 520 MeV

            TRIUMF in Vancouver, the world's largest normal-conducting cyclotron, built with over 4000 tonnes of steel. Its particles travel about 45 km while spiralling out.

            8.3 T

            The LHC at CERN is a synchrotron, not a cyclotron: 1232 superconducting dipole magnets keep protons of up to 7 TeV (its design energy) on a fixed 27 km ring.

            Model assumptions (what the lab simplifies)

            • The field B is uniform inside the dees. Real magnets have fringe fields and shaped variations that focus the beam vertically.
            • B = μ₀NI/g treats the iron as perfect and ignores saturation above about 2 T.
            • The gap is treated as thin: the particle gains its energy at the centre line of the gap.
            • The particle starts at rest at the ion source, placed so the ideal beam's outer orbits end up centred.
            • Motion is 2-D, in a perfect vacuum, for one particle. Collisions are drawn in 2-D (real events are 3-D).
            • Stopping powers use the Bethe formula without shell and density corrections (very low energies are held constant); electrons use the Katz–Penfold range formula.
            • Target heating assumes the beam heats a 1 cm² patch of each slab with slow, simple cooling.
            • Constants: CODATA 2018; particle masses from the Particle Data Group.
